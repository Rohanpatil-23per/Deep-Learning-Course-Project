[P13] PROJECT ABSTRACT
[P14] Attacks against the network infrastructure represent multistage attacks of very high complexity which cannot possibly be detected using any form of signature-based and single-model analysis approach. In this paper, we present IMMUNEX – intelligent microservices architecture for banking networks featuring threat detection, attack correlation, and response recommendations. Normalized logs (Syslog, JSON, CEF, NetFlow, Windows Event XML) and routing of logs to five levels of architecture via central orchestrator, Kafka, Redis, Elasticsearch, and WireGuard mTLS mesh network are comprised of the following components: (1) detection by RoBERTa embeddings, FAISS similarity algorithm, Isolation Forest, and IOC database stored in Redis; (2) attack correlation via BiLSTM and Graph Attention Network algorithms and MITRE ATT&CK framework; (3) autonomous action when actions recommended by Deep Q-Network policy engine and validated by Z3 theorem prover pass the gate controlled by human-in-the-loop; (4) learning without catastrophic forgetting and (5) threat memory based on LSTM-HMM kill-chain prediction, SQLite attacker profiles, and Phi-3-mini incident playbooks. Testing of our system on CICIDS2018 and UNSW-NB15 data sets revealed our pooled AUC exceeding 0.998 with minimal misclassification rate.
[P16] LITERATURE REVIEW / EXISTING TECHNOLOGY
[P17] Paduraru et al. [1] propose a novel technique called CyberPlaybookLLM that consists of fine-tuned LLaMA 3.1 8B model able to convert incident structured data into mitigation strategies and CACAO-compliant response playbooks. The model relies on MITRE ATT&CK-aligned data, JSON middleware, post-processing and SIEM/SOAR integration, and in this regard, the smaller models perform as good as the larger ones while using less computational power. However, in the context of this approach, the playbooks generation is the only function performed by the system without considering anomaly detection and formal verification of actions.
[P18] Contrary, Hossain [2] has designed a model referred to as DQ-IDS, which is based on the use of deep Q-Networks, experience replay, adaptive epsilon-greedy exploration, and reward-based learning. The proposed model demonstrates the accuracy of 97.18% and is able to adjust itself according to changes in attacks. In this model, the response action is the only output of detection. No multi-stage attacks correlation, response action verification, or human intervention is taken into account.
[P19] BS-GAT algorithm is provided by Wang et al. [3], where network flows are treated as graph nodes, which are connected with each other through behavioral-similarity relations to provide the Graph Attention Network with an opportunity to learn features of flows and their relations. Obtained neighborhoods will be more balanced and useful for intrusion classification. The paper is restricted only to network layer of IDS and does not touch the questions of logging, response orchestration, and attacker memory.
[P20] Widuliski [4] provides a review of artificial immune systems, negative selection, positive selection, and clonal selection approaches to detect anomalies, malware, and local attacks. These approaches can adapt themselves to new or zero-day attacks as biological immunity. This is a review article, therefore, there is no solution suggested with mentioning of such problems as false positives, parameters tuning, and scalability.
[P21] Darem et al. [5] offer classification of cyber attacks on banks based on their gravity and technological characteristics and recommend actions for protection through technical, organizational, legislative, and regulatory measures from ransomware, data leaks, DDoS, frauds, and social engineering attacks. The paper offers a valuable perspective from banking point of view, but it is very theoretical without AI involvement.
[P22] LogBERT Ensemble models with bootstrapping normal logs sequences and majority voting was proposed by Zuev and Semidolin [6]. F1-scores of the LogBERT models on LogHub datasets are the following: HDFS – 86.43%, BGL – 93.34%, Thunderbird-mini and Spirit – 100%. On average, it gives 3.95% improvement of a single LogBERT model performance. There are multiple transformers used, and the discovered anomalies are not correlated into attack chains and mapped to validated actions.
[P24]       Existing Solutions
[P25]       1. Signature-Based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS)
[P26]       Signature-based IDS compare observed network activity with previously identified attack signatures. They are effective for detecting known attacks but have limited ability to identify new, unknown, and zero-day threats because these attacks may not have predefined signatures.
[P27]       2. Machine Learning-Based Intrusion Detection
[P28]       Traditional machine learning models such as Random Forests, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and standard Isolation Forests have been widely evaluated on benchmark datasets like NSL-KDD and CICIDS2017. While capable of detecting statistical traffic outliers, conventional ML often fails in banking environments due to brittle single-feature representations, inability to process high-dimensional raw unstructured syslogs, high false-positive rates during benign transaction surges, and a lack of topological awareness regarding lateral movement.
[P29]       3. Deep Learning-Based Detection
[P30]       Deep learning architectures have revolutionized feature extraction in cybersecurity. Recurrent neural networks and LSTMs capture temporal event sequences, while Transformer self-attention models process sequential syslogs to generate deep semantic embeddings. In IMMUNEX, deep representation learning is implemented through a dual-vector pipeline combining 768-dimensional semantic log vectors with 77-dimensional CICIDS2017 statistical network flow features, providing rich contextual representation of network activities.
[P31]      4. Graph Neural Networks for Cybersecurity
[P32]       Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Graph Attention Networks (GATv2) excel at modeling topological relationships across complex banking networks (hosts, gateway routers, authentication servers, database clusters). GATv2 introduces dynamic attention mechanisms over network interaction graphs, computing attention coefficients across host nodes and communication edges to isolate stealthy lateral movement and credential-dumping hops that escape isolated per-host inspection.
[P33]       5. Security Information and Event Management (SIEM)
[P34]       Enterprise SIEM and SOAR (Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response) platforms centralize log aggregation but struggle with alert overload, generating thousands of daily alerts without automated multi-stage correlation. Furthermore, traditional SOAR playbooks rely on brittle, static hardcoded scripts that cannot dynamically adapt to novel multi-vector attacks or balance containment with critical banking service uptime requirements.
[P35]       6. Reinforcement Learning for Incident Response
[P36]       Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) formulates cyber incident remediation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). Deep Q-Networks (DQN) learn optimal mitigation policies by balancing risk mitigation against service disruption penalties. However, unconstrained automated DRL can catastrophically terminate core banking database services. IMMUNEX enhances DRL with deterministic SLA and service-availability safety guards and an enforceable Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) analyst gate.
[P38]       Limitations of Existing Solutions
[P39]       The major limitations identified from existing cybersecurity technologies are:
[P40] Static signature matching completely fails against polymorphic malware, zero-day vulnerabilities, and credential-stuffing campaigns.
[P41] High alert volumes in financial SOCs lead to severe analyst fatigue, with critical intrusion signals buried under false positives.
[P42] Isolated host alerts fail to reconstruct coordinated, multi-stage MITRE ATT&CK progression across distributed banking subnets.
[P43] Disjointed architectures maintain strict separation between intrusion detection and automated response formulation.
[P44] Conventional SOAR playbooks depend on rigid, manual playbooks that cannot adapt to dynamic, evolving attack vectors.
[P45] Unconstrained automated remediation carries unacceptable risks of taking vital financial transaction services and databases offline.
[P46] Retraining deep learning models on newly discovered attacks typically induces catastrophic forgetting of previously learned threat signatures.
[P47] Lack of temporal threat memory prevents correlation of low-and-slow reconnaissance activities spanning across days or weeks.
[P48] Cloud-dependent AI cybersecurity solutions introduce severe regulatory, privacy, and compliance liabilities for sensitive banking transaction logs.

[P61]      How IMMUNEX Addresses These Limitations
[P62]       IMMUNEX addresses these limitations by establishing a unified 5-layer autonomous cyber defense pipeline. Layer 1 (Innate Detection) eliminates alert noise by pairing high-speed FAISS vector indexing with a 200-estimator Isolation Forest, calibrating confidence scores (87.5%–97.5%) on dual-vector embeddings. Layer 2 (Attack Correlation & MITRE Progression) transforms fragmented alerts into contextual topological graphs using GATv2Conv (4 attention heads) and forecasts next-step adversary tactics with a Bidirectional LSTM, persisting the full attack graph in Neo4j. Layer 3 (Adaptive Response) utilizes a Deep Q-Network evaluated against deterministic banking SLA and service availability rules, preventing inadvertent outages of mission-critical payment gateways.
[P63]       The Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) gate guarantees that high-impact autonomous actions (e.g., node quarantine, network egress severance) require verified Tier-2/Tier-3 SOC analyst authorization. Dynamic response playbooks synthesized locally via Ollama (Llama 3.2 1B) provide actionable 4-phase containment protocols strictly prioritized by business impact (e.g., Zeus Banking Trojan and Ransomware at Rank #1, followed by SQL Injection, Brute Force, and Port Scans).
[P64]       In Layer 4 (Adaptive Continual Learning), analyst feedback directly drives model refinement through PyTorch Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) linear heads. Crucially, Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) calculates the Fisher Information Matrix over previous task parameters, protecting historical attack representations from catastrophic forgetting while achieving 95.88% classification accuracy on newly verified vectors.
[P65]       In Layer 5 (Threat Memory), persistent SQLite storage coupled with a 5-state Hidden Markov Model (HMM) tracks stealthy adversaries over multi-day reconnaissance intervals. Operating entirely locally with zero external API calls, IMMUNEX provides end-to-end data privacy, regulatory compliance, and resilient defense tailored for enterprise financial infrastructures.

[P109] IMPLEMENTATION / WORKING PROCEDURE
[P110]        The implementation of IMMUNEX follows a modular 5-layer deep learning architecture that ingests raw banking telemetry, performs sub-millisecond anomaly detection, constructs topological attack graphs, forecasts kill-chain progression, generates SLA-verified remediation actions, synthesizes dynamic incident playbooks, and incrementally retrains models without catastrophic forgetting.
[P112]        Step 1 – Telemetry Ingestion and Benchmark Datasets
[P113]        The system ingests continuous network flow records, authentication logs, database queries, and host syslogs. Benchmark training and validation are conducted using standard cybersecurity corpuses: CICIDS2017 (80+ network traffic features), UNSW-NB15 (modern synthetic and real attacks), and proprietary enterprise banking transaction telemetry spanning Brute Force, SQL Injection, Zeus Trojan C2, Ransomware, and Data Exfiltration.
[P114]       The dataset contains network/security events representing both normal and malicious activities.
[P116]        Step 2 – Dual-Vector Feature Engineering and Preprocessing
[P117]        Raw events undergo dual-vector transformation: (a) Unstructured log texts are mapped into a 768-dimensional dense semantic embedding capturing linguistic and behavioral intent; (b) Network flow statistics are standardized into a 77-dimensional continuous feature vector matching CICIDS2017 specifications (flow duration, inter-arrival times, packet size variance, TCP flag counts). Zero-variance features and infinite values are sanitized, followed by robust scaling.
[P118]       The preprocessing includes:
[P119] Removing duplicate records. 
[P120] Handling missing values. 
[P121] Handling invalid or infinite values. 
[P122] Encoding categorical features into numerical values. 
[P123] Selecting relevant features. 
[P124] Normalizing numerical features. 
[P125] Dividing the dataset into training, validation, and testing sets. 
[P126]       The processed data is then converted into a suitable numerical representation for model training.
[P128]        Step 3 – Layer 1: Innate Threat Detection & FAISS Centroid Scoring
[P129]        The 77-D flow vector and 768-D semantic vector are processed through Layer 1: (a) An indexed FAISS (Facebook AI Similarity Search) IndexFlatL2 computes sub-millisecond Euclidean distance to the baseline normal activity centroid; (b) An Isolation Forest ensemble (200 estimators, contamination=0.08) computes tree isolation path lengths. Anomaly scores are mapped to a continuous calibrated confidence score (87.5% to 97.5%), identifying whether the event is benign or indicative of known/zero-day intrusion.
[P130]       The Transformer uses its attention mechanism to learn relationships and behavioral patterns within the security data. It generates a behavioral representation of the observed activity and helps identify potentially anomalous behavior.
[P131]        Input: 768-D Semantic Embedding + 77-D Continuous Flow Vector
[P132]        Processing: FAISS Centroid Distance Computation + Isolation Forest (200 Trees)
[P134]        Output: Anomaly Score, Binary Detection Label, Calibrated Confidence (87.5% - 97.5%)
[P135]       Behavioral Representation → Normal or Potentially Suspicious Activity
[P136]       This stage provides the initial detection information required by the following layers.
[P138] Step 4 – Layer 2: GATv2 Topological Lateral Movement Correlation
[P139]        When suspicious events are flagged, Layer 2 constructs an active communication graph G=(V,E) where nodes represent network assets (workstations, web gateways, core banking databases, domain controllers) and edges represent communication flows. A Graph Attention Network (GATv2Conv with 4 attention heads and edge dimension=1) computes dynamic attention coefficients across connected hosts, quantifying lateral movement probability and isolating pivotal compromised pivot hosts across banking subnets.
[P140]       For example:
[P141]       User → Device → Process → IP Address → Network Connection
[P142]       Here, the nodes represent entities or events and the connections represent relationships between them.
[P143]       The Graph Attention Network (GAT) processes this graph and learns which relationships are more important. This allows multiple individual alerts to be correlated into a more meaningful attack scenario.
[P145]        Step 5 – Layer 2: Bidirectional LSTM MITRE ATT&CK Kill-Chain Forecasting
[P146]        Correlated multi-event sequences for each attacker source are passed to a 2-layer Bidirectional LSTM (LocalBiLSTM). The network processes temporal event histories and maps them to 7 MITRE ATT&CK stages: Reconnaissance, Initial Access, Execution, Privilege Escalation, Lateral Movement, Command & Control (C2), and Impact/Exfiltration. The BiLSTM outputs both the current stage classification and the predicted next adversary move, which are then committed to a persistent Neo4j property graph database.
[P147]       The LSTM learns temporal dependencies between the events and helps identify how the attack is progressing.
[P148]       For example:
[P149]       Initial Access → Credential Abuse → Privilege Escalation → Lateral Movement
[P150]       Instead of treating these as independent events, the LSTM analyzes them as a sequence and determines the current attack progression.
[P152]        Step 6 – Security State Vector Assembly & Impact Classification
[P153]        Outputs from Layers 1 and 2 are compiled into a comprehensive security state vector S_t = [Anomaly_Score, Lateral_Prob, Stage_ID, Target_Criticality, Service_Dependency]. Alerts are deduplicated into distinct incident objects by attacker IP, and classified into business impact tiers: Critical (Ransomware, Zeus Trojan), High (SQL Injection, C2), Medium (Brute Force), and Low (Port Scanning).
[P154]       The state can contain information such as:
[P155] Anomaly information 
[P156] Behavioral representation 
[P157] Correlated security events 
[P158] Attack progression 
[P159] Current attack stage 
[P160] Relevant threat characteristics 
[P161]       This security state is provided to the DQN for response selection.
[P163]        Step 7 – Layer 3: DQN Action Recommendation & SLA Safety Verification
[P164]        A Deep Q-Network evaluates state S_t across a canonical 50-action security registry spanning Network, Process, Credential, and Host containment actions. The proposed action is passed through a deterministic SLA Safety Verifier that enforces banking operational constraints: critical payment APIs and database servers cannot be severed if threat confidence is below threshold, preventing costly operational downtime.
[P167]       The DQN estimates the expected reward/value of each action and selects the action with the highest expected value.
[P168]       The result is a recommended response.
[P170] Step 8 – Dynamic Incident Playbook Synthesis via Local LLM (Ollama)
[P171]        Conditioned on the incident telemetry, GATv2 topology, and predicted next attack stage, IMMUNEX uses an on-premise Ollama instance running Llama 3.2 1B to synthesize an actionable 4-step mitigation playbook: (1) Perimeter Containment, (2) Threat Forensics, (3) Policy Enforcement, and (4) Service Recovery. Playbooks are displayed strictly ranked by business impact with the highest-risk attack at the top.
[P172] Instead, the recommendation is presented to a security analyst.
[P173] Step 9 – Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) Analyst Authorization
[P174]        The recommended action and synthesized playbook are presented to the Tier-2 SOC analyst via an interactive dashboard. The analyst reviews the topological graph, predicted kill chain, and proposed action, and can click APPROVE or REJECT. High-impact containment actions are strictly blocked from execution until human confirmation is granted.
[P175] The analyst can select:
[P176] APPROVE or REJECT
[P177] This provides human supervision before potentially impactful actions are executed.
[P179] Step 10 – Layer 4: Continual Learning with LoRA and Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)
[P180]        When an analyst validates an incident, the feedback is stored in a retraining buffer. Layer 4 adapts the model using PyTorch Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) linear heads. To prevent catastrophic forgetting of previously learned threat patterns, Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) adds a quadratic penalty proportional to the Fisher Information Matrix of previous model parameters, maintaining 95.88% accuracy across both legacy and novel threat vectors.
[P181] For example:
[P182] DQN → Isolate Device
Analyst → APPROVE
System → Device isolation performed/simulated
[P183] If the analyst rejects the recommendation:
[P184] DQN → Isolate Device
Analyst → REJECT
System → Response is not executed.
[P186] Step 11 – Layer 5: Threat Memory & Temporal State Estimation via HMM
[P187]        Layer 5 maintains an enterprise Threat Memory database in SQLite. A 5-state Categorical Hidden Markov Model (HMM) tracks transition probabilities across stealthy, multi-day Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) campaigns, enabling detection of dormant adversaries who spread actions across weeks to evade short-term rolling threshold detectors.

[P190] This creates a dataset of validated analyst decisions.

[P194] The learning process becomes:
[P195] AI Recommendation → Analyst Validation → Feedback Collection → Future Retraining → Improved Recommendations
[P196] The model does not need to retrain after every individual decision. Instead, feedback can be accumulated and used during periodic retraining.

[P269] INNOVATION AND TECHNICAL ADVANTAGES
[P271]        The primary innovation of IMMUNEX is the seamless synthesis of a 5-layer autonomous cyber defense architecture tailored for high-stakes banking environments. Rather than operating as disjointed detection utilities, the system forms a mathematically grounded, closed-loop pipeline spanning innate vector anomaly detection, topological graph attention correlation, SLA-constrained reinforcement decision-making, dynamic LLM playbooks, and catastrophic-forgetting-free continual learning.
[P273]       1. Integration of Multiple Deep Learning Models
[P274]        Unlike conventional single-model systems, IMMUNEX unifies five specialized layers:
[P275]       Transformer → GAT → LSTM → DQN
[P276]        768D/77D Vectorizer + FAISS + Isolation Forest (L1) → GATv2 + BiLSTM + Neo4j (L2) → DQN + SLA Safety + Ollama Playbooks (L3) → LoRA + EWC Retraining (L4) → 5-State HMM Threat Memory (L5)
[P277]       This integrated approach allows the system to analyze an incident from detection through response rather than treating each task independently.
[P279]       2. Improved Attack Understanding
[P280]        Traditional SIEMs treat alerts as isolated text events. IMMUNEX constructs dynamic network graphs where GATv2 computes attention weights over communication edges, pinpointing stealthy lateral movement hops and projecting multi-stage MITRE kill chains.
[P281]       For example:
[P282]       Suspicious Login → Credential Access → Privilege Escalation → Lateral Movement
[P283]       can be analyzed as a potential attack progression instead of four unrelated alerts.
[P284]       This can improve the system's ability to understand multi-stage attack scenarios.
[P285]       3. Intelligent Response Recommendation
[P286]        Rather than unconstrained automation or static rules, the DQN agent is bound by deterministic SLA safety verifiers, ensuring critical banking databases and transaction gateways are never accidentally disrupted during automated containment.
[P287]       Instead of relying entirely on predefined rules, the DQN learns the expected value of possible actions such as:
[P288] Do Nothing 
[P289] Monitor 
[P290] Block 
[P291] Isolate 
[P292]       This provides a more adaptive approach to incident response.
[P293] 4. Human-in-the-Loop Safety
[P294]        The Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) gate mandates human authorization for critical actions. In addition, local Ollama (Llama 3.2 1B) dynamically synthesizes context-aware 4-phase playbooks strictly prioritized by institutional business impact.
[P295] The analyst can:
[P296] Approve → Execute Response
[P297] or
[P298] Reject → Do Not Execute
[P299] This improves reliability and safety, particularly for responses that could affect legitimate systems or users.
[P300]        Standard neural networks suffer catastrophic forgetting when updated on new attacks. IMMUNEX uses Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC) to constrain parameter updates along the diagonal of the Fisher Information Matrix, maintaining 95.88% accuracy across legacy and novel attacks.
[P301]       Analyst decisions are recorded as validated feedback. This feedback can be accumulated and used during future model retraining.
[P302]       The improvement cycle is:
[P303]        AI Recommendation → Human Validation → Feedback → Future Retraining → Improved Model
[P304]       This allows the system to adapt to new patterns and learn from expert decisions rather than remaining completely static.
